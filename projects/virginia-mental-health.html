Geographic Disparities in Mental Health Across Virginia Counties

Where frequent mental distress is highest in Virginia, and how it relates to the supply of mental health providers, and how childhood poverty relates to mentally unhealthy days, mapped and analyzed in Tableau.

Course
HLTH 652 Data Visualization & Spatial Analysis
Data
2025 County Health Rankings, Virginia
Design
Secondary ecological analysis
Tool
Tableau

Ifeoluwanimi P. Shobayo (first author), Marylyn O. Oduneye, Tahirou Diallo, Fadzai G. Nyarugwe, Chelsea R. Mazonde, Cynthia C. Ilechukwu. Group poster; its topic, dataset, and study questions follow my individual HLTH 652 Data Visualization Project proposal (January 2026).

Research question

How do mental health outcomes and provider availability vary across Virginia counties, and how are provider availability and childhood poverty associated with county-level mental health burden?

Methods

  • Measures: % frequent mental distress, average mentally unhealthy days, age-adjusted suicide rate, mental health providers per 100,000, % children in poverty, rurality, and population.
  • Analysis in Tableau: a county choropleth map, descriptive comparisons, counties grouped into four provider-availability tiers, and linear trend analyses.

Key findings

FindingResult
Frequent mental distress across counties13.5%–23.6%, highest in southwestern and southside Virginia
Provider availability vs. frequent distressNegative association, R² = 0.08, p = 0.002
Average county age-adjusted suicide rate (per 100,000), very-low (<50 providers per 100,000) vs. high (300+) provider tiers20.0 vs. 15.2 (about 31% higher; descriptive, unadjusted comparison, no significance test reported)
Childhood poverty vs. mentally unhealthy daysPositive association, R² = 0.51, p < 0.0001

Provider supply explained only a small share of the variation in distress (8%). Childhood poverty showed a stronger association with mentally unhealthy days (R² = 0.51). These are separate, unadjusted, cross-sectional county-level associations, so they show where burden, poverty, and provider supply overlap, not what would reduce distress; together they support considering both behavioral health access and socioeconomic conditions.

Limitations: county-level (ecological) data can't describe individual risk, the data are cross-sectional, so they show associations at one point in time, not cause and effect; factors not adjusted for (including rurality and other demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare factors) may explain the differences; and provider rates measure supply, not affordability or actual use. Future work proposed on the poster includes multivariable analysis, multi-year trends, and broader access measures.

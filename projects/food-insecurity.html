Food Insecurity Across U.S. States, 2019–2023

How food insecurity changed through the pandemic years, where it is highest, and which measures move with it, using five years of state-level data analyzed in SAS.

Course
PUBH 630 Statistical Software Lab
Data
Feeding America Map the Meal Gap
Observations
255 state-years (50 states + DC × 5 years)
Tools
SAS Studio, Tableau Prep, Excel

Group project with Cynthia Ilechukwu and Okechukwu Nwajiaku · May 2026

Objectives

  1. Describe national trends in state food insecurity from 2019 to 2023.
  2. Compare rates across Census regions and identify high-rate states.
  3. Measure how overall food insecurity relates to subgroup rates, SNAP-threshold measures, and food costs.
  4. Be clear about what this dataset can and cannot show.

From raw file to analysis

Feeding America's research team approved our use of the data. We kept state-level records, used Tableau Prep to isolate the key variables, and analyzed them in SAS Studio:

Import the CSVPROC IMPORT

The 2020–2021 low coincided with pandemic relief such as expanded SNAP benefits and stimulus payments. By 2023, after most of that aid had ended, the average was higher than before the pandemic. The dataset has no benefit or price variables, so these explanations remain hypotheses.

*Labeled as an average in the original report; the values match national totals.

Where and for whom

  • Region: By Census region, the South (e.g., Mississippi, Alabama, Louisiana, Arkansas) had the highest median rate across 2019–2023 (about 13.5%) and the Northeast the lowest (about 10.5%), with the West and Midwest in between. Ranges overlapped considerably across regions.
  • Race and ethnicity: food insecurity among Black residents was consistently about 2–3 times the rate among White non-Hispanic residents, and rates among Hispanic residents were also elevated.

What moves with overall food insecurity?

Pearson correlation with the overall rate

255 state-year observations, PROC CORR

Child food insecurity was very strongly correlated with the overall rate (r = 0.92), though this largely reflects how the measures are built: subgroup rates are partly components of the overall rate. Cost per meal showed essentially no linear correlation with the overall rate (r = −0.05 across state-years). Because the 255 observations are 51 states measured five times, these correlations are descriptive rather than formal tests.

Limitations and next steps

  • State-level associations only: no conclusions about individuals and no causal claims.
  • Key drivers are missing, including poverty, unemployment, income, rent burden, and SNAP participation.
  • Five years is too short to separate temporary shocks from long-term trends.
  • Next step: link state-year policy data (e.g., SNAP participation, emergency allotments) and USDA's survey-based state estimates. Map the Meal Gap rates are themselves modeled from poverty, unemployment, and income, so regressing them on those same variables would mostly recover the model's inputs.

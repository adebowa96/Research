Research Proposal · Epidemiology

Stress, Anxiety, and Social Support Among Graduate Students

A cross-sectional study design comparing mental-health outcomes between international and domestic graduate students at a faith-based university.

Course
HLTH 511 Research Methods in Public Health
Design
Cross-sectional survey
Target sample
120–150 students
Date
November 2025

Research question

How do stress, anxiety, and depression differ between international and domestic graduate students, and how might perceived social support and campus services influence these differences?

Why it matters

Graduate students report higher rates of depression, anxiety, and burnout than the general adult population. International students face extra pressures, including acculturative stress, language barriers, and distance from family, but often use counseling services less because of stigma, confidentiality concerns, and unfamiliarity with U.S. care systems.

The literature consistently links higher perceived social support to lower distress and that campus context shapes help-seeking. Few studies, however, compare international and domestic graduate students in faith-based universities while also measuring social support and use of campus services. This study is designed to fill that gap.

Study design

I chose a quantitative cross-sectional survey with three open-ended questions. This design compares groups and identifies correlates of distress efficiently, without the cost of long-term follow-up, though it cannot show which came first.

  • Population: enrolled graduate students aged 18 or older, recruited through the Graduate Student Office and International Student Center.
  • Sample size: 120–150 participants, including at least 50 international students. At n = 120, a two-group comparison has about 77% power for moderate effects (d ≈ 0.5), reaching 80% at about 128 students (64 per group); detecting a smaller effect (d ≈ 0.35) would need about 130 students per group.
  • Robustness: Welch's t-tests (no equal-variance assumption, which matters most when group sizes are unequal) and bootstrapped confidence intervals for skewed DASS-21 scores.
  • Pilot test: 5–8 participants, including at least one international student, to check that the questions are clear and easy to understand. Items are written in culturally neutral language.

Analysis plan

  1. Descriptive statistics to summarize the sample and outcome scores.
  2. Independent-samples t-tests (Welch's version, which does not assume equal variances and is robust when group sizes also differ) to compare international and domestic students.
  3. Multiple regression to estimate the adjusted difference in DASS-21 scores between international and domestic students, controlling for the covariates listed above.
  4. Moderation analysis (student status × perceived support) to test whether the link between social support and distress differs between international and domestic students.
  5. Thematic analysis of open-ended answers to add context to the numbers.
  6. Missing data: listwise deletion if under 5%; multiple imputation or the EM algorithm if 5% or more.

Ethics and data protection

The study would require IRB approval and electronic informed consent. Responses would be anonymous, with no names or IP addresses collected, and data would be stored securely. Because the survey asks about mental health, every participant would see a debriefing page with counseling and 24/7 crisis resources.

Expected outcomes

  • International students may report similar or higher stress but lower rates of diagnosis and service use.
  • Across all students, higher perceived social support is expected to go with lower stress, anxiety, and depression.
  • The findings would guide culturally responsive support from counseling, international student, and academic services.

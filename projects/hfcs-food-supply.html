High-Fructose Corn Syrup in the U.S. Branded Food Supply

How many U.S. branded food products list high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS) as an ingredient? A cross-sectional analysis of the USDA Global Branded Food Products Database.

Venue
APHA 2026 Annual Meeting and Expo (accepted poster)
Data
USDA FoodData Central Global Branded Food Products Database, 2019–2025
Design
Retrospective, cross-sectional, descriptive
My role
Co-author

Miebaka F. Roberts (first author), Chelsea R. Mazonde, Ifeoluwanimi P. Shobayo

Why this question

HFCS is a widely used caloric sweetener in soft drinks, baked goods, ready-to-eat meals, and condiments. Ingredient lists don’t give amounts, and nutrient databases don’t separate types of added sugar, so there is little product-level data on how common HFCS is. The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines state that no amount of added sugars is recommended as part of a healthy diet.

What proportion of U.S. branded food products list HFCS as an ingredient?

Methods

  • Data: secondary analysis of the USDA FoodData Central Global Branded Food Products Database, an industry-supplied database of branded and private-label products. No new data were collected.
  • Window: products published April 13, 2019 (earliest available) to December 13, 2025 (search date), across all trade channels.
  • Identifying HFCS: the ingredients field was searched for “High,” “Fructose,” “Corn,” and “Syrup” (all four terms required).
  • Analysis: counts and percentages in Microsoft Excel.
454,324 products identified
6,717 excluded: “Drug” category and New Zealand market
447,607 eligible U.S. branded products
29,503 list HFCS as an ingredient (6.6%)

Results

HFCS was listed in 29,503 of 447,607 eligible products (6.6%), about 1 in 15. The other 418,104 (93.4%) did not list it. A 6.6% product share doesn’t mean low exposure: counts aren’t weighted by sales, and HFCS may cluster in high-consumption foods such as sweetened beverages, desserts, and condiments.

Limitations: the analysis measures whether HFCS is listed, not how much a product contains or how much people eat; data quality varies by industry contributor; the search could over-count (terms need not be adjacent) or under-count (other names such as “glucose-fructose syrup” are missed); and package sizes are counted as separate items.

Public health implications

  • Use ingredient-level surveillance to track HFCS and other added sugars in branded foods.
  • Target reformulation and inform procurement standards in food service and child nutrition programs.
  • Link ingredient data to sales data, and stratify by food category, to estimate actual exposure.

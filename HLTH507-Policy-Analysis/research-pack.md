## 3. Candidate A: Into the Light Act (federal, 2022)

**Full name:** Into the Light for Maternal Mental Health and Substance Use Disorders Act of 2022 (H.R. 7073 / S. 3824), enacted within the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 (Pub L 117-328).

### What it does
- **Authorizes the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline**: 1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262). Free, confidential, 24/7 call and text support for pregnant and postpartum people and their families, in English and Spanish, with interpreters in 60+ languages.
- **Reauthorizes and expands HRSA grants to states** to screen and treat maternal mental health and substance use disorders. Advocates report authorized funding rising from $10.5 million in FY2022 to $170 million total over FY2023–2028. †
- The same division of the law (Division FF, §1113) directed HHS to form a **Task Force on Maternal Mental Health**.

### Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 2016 | 21st Century Cures Act §10005 (the "Bringing Postpartum Depression Out of the Shadows Act") creates the first federal grants for maternal depression screening *and* treatment: $5 million a year for FY2018–2022, with a 20% state match (42 USC 247b-13a) |
| May 8, 2022 | HRSA launches the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline on Mother's Day |
| June 2022 | House passes H.R. 7073 (lead sponsor Rep. Katherine Clark) |
| Dec 29, 2022 | Into the Light enacted within the year-end Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 |
| First year | Hotline handles nearly 13,400 contacts |
| May 14, 2024 | Task Force releases the first **National Strategy to Improve Maternal Mental Health Care** and a Report to Congress. Five pillars, including "Lift Up Lived Experience" |
| Mar–Apr 2025 | HHS reduction in force under Executive Order 14210. HRSA loses 4 of the 6 full-time staff who oversee the hotline. Sens. Gillibrand and Blunt Rochester demand answers from Secretary Kennedy |
| 2025 | FY2026 budget: President proposes $7 million for the hotline; House and Senate committees propose $8 million and direct more outreach to service members, veterans and military families |
| Aug 13, 2026 | GAO report: hotline answer rates of 92%–95% a month in FY2025 and rising contact volume; the 4 oversight positions still unfilled as of March 2026 |

### Key actors
- **House sponsors:** Katherine Clark (D-MA), Jaime Herrera Beutler (R-WA), Doris Matsui (D-CA), Michael Burgess (R-TX), Yvette Clarke (D-NY), Young Kim (R-CA)
- **Senate sponsors:** Kirsten Gillibrand (D-NY), Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV), Tammy Baldwin (D-WI), Lisa Murkowski (R-AK)
- **Advocates:** Maternal Mental Health Leadership Alliance (MMHLA), 2020 Mom, Postpartum Support International
- **Agencies:** HRSA (hotline and grants), SAMHSA (Task Force)

### Angles for the assignment
- **Process:** A bipartisan bill that became law inside a huge end-of-year spending package rather than on its own. The hotline was launched by the agency (May 2022) *before* Congress put it into law. Implementation was then shaped by a new administration's workforce cuts.
- **Evaluation:** GAO measured operations (answer rates, volume), not whether mothers got better. That gap is a ready-made "has it worked as intended?" question.
- **Subpopulations:** military families (Congress's own directive), rural mothers, Black mothers, mothers with substance use disorders, Spanish speakers.

## 4. Candidate B: California screening laws (2018 and 2024)

### What the laws do
- **AB 2193** (Asm. Brian Maienschein), signed Sept 26, 2018; in effect July 1, 2019. Obstetric providers must confirm a patient was screened for maternal mental health conditions at least once during pregnancy or postpartum, or screen her themselves. Health plans and insurers must create maternal mental health programs with case management.
- **AB 1936** (Asm. Cervantes), signed Sept 28, 2024; in effect Jan 1, 2025. Plans must cover at least one screening during pregnancy, one in the first 6 weeks postpartum, and more when medically necessary.

### Angles for the assignment
- **Process:** Advocates (2020 Mom) called AB 2193 a "unique approach" because it paired screening with plan-run case management. AB 1936 revised the rules six years later by spelling out how many screenings plans must cover. That's a clean example of the "policy modification" stage.
- **Evidence debate:** NPR (2018) on the pros and cons of mandatory screening; KFF Health News on doctors with "nowhere to send" mothers who screen positive.
- **Equity:** State mandates don't reach self-funded employer plans (ERISA), so some Californians aren't covered. Medi-Cal and commercial plans can also differ.
- **Lived experience:** CalMatters (2019) covered Wendy Root Askew, who had postpartum depression and backed AB 2193, and a family affected by postpartum psychosis.

## 5. Candidate C: New York screening law (2024) and state report (2025)

### What the law does
**S2039-B / A2870-B** (Sen. Samra Brouk), signed by Gov. Kathy Hochul in December 2024. The Department of Health, working with the state's mental health and addiction agencies, must publish guidance for depression screening during pregnancy and after birth: when to start, how often to screen, and how to refer. The guidance must cover screening for social needs linked to maternal depression: social support, intimate partner violence, food and housing insecurity, diaper insecurity, and barriers to care. The department must also post links to training on equity, bias, cultural competency, screening, treatment, patients' rights, and trauma-informed care. **† Chapter number to confirm.**

### Related New York developments
- **Nov 6, 2025:** Gov. Hochul announced New York's first-ever maternal mental health report (128 pages), from a workgroup created by the FY2024 state budget. Recommendations include expanding Project TEACH consultation for OB-GYNs and pediatricians, integrating behavioral health into OB care through Collaborative Care, universal screening with validated tools, and Medicaid doula benefits. The report says mental health contributed to **19.5%** of pregnancy-related deaths in New York.
- **2025 bill A1025 / S802** appears to amend the 2024 law's guidance requirements. † Check its status.
- **Earlier history:** Gov. Cuomo signed an earlier maternal depression bill. † Year to confirm. This would give a longer New York timeline.

## 6. Shared evidence base

These work for any of the three candidates. Numbers in brackets point to §12.

| Finding | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Leading cause of pregnancy-related deaths | Mental health conditions, including suicide and overdose: **22.7%** | CDC maternal mortality review committees, 36 states, 2017–2019 [1] |
| When deaths happen | **53%** occurred 7–365 days after birth; **30%** in days 43–365 | [1] |
| Postpartum depressive symptoms | **13.2%** overall; from 9.7% (Illinois) to 23.5% (Mississippi) | CDC PRAMS, 2018 [2] |
| Not asked about depression | **1 in 5** not asked during prenatal visits; **1 in 8** not asked postpartum | [2] |
| How common | Perinatal depression affects as many as **1 in 7** women | USPSTF, 2019 [3] |
| Cost of not treating | **$14.2 billion** for 2017 births (pregnancy to age 5); about **$31,800** per mother–child pair | Luca et al., 2020 [4] |
| Treatment cascade (postpartum depression) | 30.8% identified → 15.8% treated → 6.3% adequately treated → **3.2%** reach remission | Cox et al., 2016 [5] |
| Screening mandate alone | New Jersey's 2006 mandate did **not** improve treatment start, follow-up or continuation for women on Medicaid | Kozhimannil et al., 2011 [6] |
| Racial gaps | NJ Medicaid: **9%** of white, **4%** of Black and **5%** of Latina mothers started postpartum mental health care | Kozhimannil et al., 2011 [7] |
| Prevention works | Counseling (cognitive behavioral or interpersonal therapy) for at-risk women: **grade B** | USPSTF, 2019 [3] |
| Professional standard | Screen for depression and anxiety with validated tools at the first prenatal visit, later in pregnancy, and postpartum | ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 4, 2023 [8] |
| Fear of child protective services | Some Black and biracial young mothers didn't report postpartum symptoms for fear of CPS involvement and unfair treatment | Dwarakanath et al., 2023 [9] |

| New York | Mental health contributed to **19.5%** of pregnancy-related deaths | NY Office of Mental Health, 2025 [12] |

**Note on comparing numbers:** The national 22.7% and New York's 19.5% come from different datasets and years. Don't present them as a direct comparison.

## 7. Evidence misuse and logical fallacies

The rubric's "Advanced" level asks you to identify "logical fallacies and poor data interpretations." Each example below is documented in the sources or follows directly from them.

1. **"Screening is the solution" (false cause).** Screening mandates assume that finding depression leads to treatment. New Jersey's 2006 mandate didn't change treatment for Medicaid mothers [6], and its evaluators said the policy was "predicated on an inadequate base of evidence." The treatment cascade shows most identified women never get adequate treatment [5]. Doctors have reported having "nowhere to send" mothers who screen positive (KFF Health News). This applies to all three candidates.
2. **Counting outputs as outcomes.** Hotline answer rates and contact counts show the service is running, not that mothers are healthier. The 2026 GAO report measures operations only [10]. Watch for officials citing contact volume as proof of impact.
3. **Cherry-picking and one-sided evidence.** At the FDA's July 21, 2025 roundtable on SSRIs in pregnancy, panelists stressed possible medication risks. ACOG's president called it "alarmingly unbalanced" because it "did not adequately acknowledge the harms of untreated perinatal mood disorders." The Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine warned that misinformation creates "unnecessary barriers to care." NPR reported the panel "spread misinformation."
4. **Appeal to fear / slippery slope.** Opponents of the earlier federal MOTHERS Act argued it would push psychotropic drugs on new mothers.
5. **Anecdote as evidence (hasty generalization).** Personal stories, like Mary Jo Codey's in New Jersey and Melanie Blocker Stokes's in Illinois, rightly put the issue on the agenda. But a moving story doesn't show which intervention works. New Jersey acted on the story before the evidence.
6. **Misreading the numbers.** "22.7% of pregnancy-related deaths" does not mean "22.7% of mothers." The CDC data cover 36 states, not the whole country.
7. **Budget framing that leaves out costs.** Staff and funding cuts can be described as savings, while untreated perinatal mood and anxiety disorders cost an estimated $14.2 billion for one year's births [4]. Present this as a question to test, not an accusation.

## 8. Ethics and biblical integration

### Principles
- **Autonomy:** Screening should be voluntary and informed. Mothers should know how their answers will be used.
- **Beneficence and nonmaleficence:** Screening with no path to treatment can raise distress and expectations without delivering help.
- **Justice:** Treatment gaps by race and income [7]; rural access; differences by insurance type.
- **Privacy:** Hotline confidentiality; how screening results are recorded and shared.

### Unintended consequences to weigh
- Fear of child protective services may keep mothers, especially Black, low-income or substance-using mothers, from answering honestly [9].
- New demand without new providers means longer waits and more burden on OBs and pediatricians.
- Uneven reach: state insurance mandates skip self-funded employer plans; the hotline depends on awareness, language and phone access.
- Who's left out: partners, adoptive parents, mothers after pregnancy loss. Check each policy's definitions.
- Stigma or labeling if screening happens without follow-up care.
- Mixed public messages about medication, such as the 2025 FDA panel, may lead some mothers to stop treatment.

### Bible passages and how they fit (ESV)
- **1 Samuel 1:10–16.** Hannah, "a woman troubled in spirit," was misjudged by the priest Eli as drunk. Distress is easily misread; screening needs compassion, not suspicion. Links to the fear-of-CPS issue.
- **1 Kings 19:3–8.** Elijah, in despair, asks to die. God first gives him sleep and food: "Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you." Supports whole-person care that meets physical and social needs, like New York's social-needs screening.
- **Psalm 34:18.** "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit."
- **Isaiah 40:11.** God "will gently lead those that are with young."
- **Galatians 6:2.** "Bear one another's burdens." Peer support, family, the hotline.
- **Proverbs 31:8–9.** "Defend the rights of the poor and needy." Equity and advocacy.
- **Genesis 1:27.** Every mother bears God's image, regardless of race or income.
- **Luke 10:25–37.** The Good Samaritan: care that crosses group lines. Ties to unintended populations.
- **Colossians 4:14.** "Luke the beloved physician." Faith and medical care aren't in conflict, which helps against stigma about treatment.

**Caution:** Avoid implying that depression is a failure of faith. The rubric marks down "misapplied biblical concepts."

## 9. Frameworks for the policy-making process

- **Kingdon's multiple streams** [20]. A policy passes when three streams meet in a "policy window":
  - *Problem stream:* CDC death-review data; high-profile deaths.
  - *Policy stream:* ready solutions such as hotlines, screening, grants, and consultation programs like MCPAP for Moms.
  - *Politics stream:* bipartisan support, budget cycles, changes in administration.
  - *Examples of windows:* the year-end spending package in Dec 2022 (Candidate A); New York's budget-mandated workgroup (Candidate C).
  - *Policy entrepreneurs:* MMHLA and 2020 Mom, legislators like Clark and Brouk, mothers with lived experience.
- **Policy stages:** agenda setting → formulation → adoption → implementation → evaluation → modification. Candidate B shows modification (AB 2193 → AB 1936). Candidate A shows implementation under a change of administration.
- Also check the course textbook for its preferred model, and cite it alongside Kingdon.

## 11. Program ideas for the Policy Design part

| Program model | Evidence | Fits which subpopulation |
|---|---|---|
| **Perinatal psychiatry access program** (MCPAP for Moms model): phone consultation for OBs, training, referral help | Byatt et al., 2018 [11]; New York's Project TEACH is similar | Rural mothers; practices without psychiatrists |
| **ROSE program**: four 90-minute prenatal group sessions on stress, social support and warning signs | Randomized trials with low-income women prevented about half of postpartum depression cases; cited by USPSTF | Low-income and Medicaid pregnant women |
| **Collaborative care in OB clinics** | Recommended in New York's 2025 report | Women who screen positive in OB settings |
| **Doulas trained in mental health and harm reduction** | Recommended in New York's 2025 report | Black mothers; mothers with substance use disorders |
| **Targeted hotline outreach** | Congress directed outreach to military families in FY2026 appropriations | Military spouses and service members |
| **Non-punitive disclosure protocols** (clear, published rules on when CPS is involved) | Dwarakanath et al., 2023 on fear of CPS [9] | Black and low-income mothers; mothers with substance use disorders |

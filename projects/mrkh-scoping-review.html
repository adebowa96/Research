Mental Health and Psychosocial Outcomes in MRKH Syndrome

A scoping review of 35 studies on how Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) syndrome affects mental health, how people cope, and where health systems fall short.

Venue
APHA 2026 Annual Meeting and Expo (accepted poster)
Method
Arksey & O'Malley · PRISMA-ScR
Studies
516 records → 35 included
Literature window
January 2019 – March 2026

Ifeoluwanimi P. Shobayo (first author), Tahirou Diallo, Chelsea Mazonde, Fadzai Nyarugwe, Oluwatosin Oduneye, Dangpa Wndji Nana, Robyn Anderson · Liberty University

Why this question

MRKH is a rare congenital condition, affecting about 1 in 4,500–5,000 female births, in which the uterus and upper vagina are absent. It is usually diagnosed in adolescence, a sensitive time for identity, sexuality, and relationships. Guidelines recommend psychosocial counseling, but earlier reviews did not map coping strategies or health-system gaps across the growing recent literature.

What mental health outcomes and coping mechanisms are reported among individuals with MRKH syndrome, and what healthcare system gaps exist?

Methods

  • Framework: Arksey & O'Malley's five-stage scoping review, reported with PRISMA-ScR.
  • Databases: PubMed, Scopus, and EBSCOhost (MEDLINE, CINAHL, APA PsycInfo, Women's Studies International).
  • Eligibility: English-language primary quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods studies reporting psychological outcomes or coping in MRKH. Mixed samples were included only if MRKH results were reported separately or at least 80% of participants had MRKH.
  • Screening: 516 records → 192 duplicates removed → 324 screened → 42 assessed → 35 included.
  • Synthesis: data charted from full texts (abstracts for 5 studies), then frequency counts and thematic grouping into six domains.

What the evidence covers

Outcome domains reported across the 35 studies

A study could report more than one domain.

  • Psychosexual and relational challenges dominated, including lower sexual esteem and more negative genital self-image than controls.
  • Psychological distress (20 studies): shock, shame, and secrecy at diagnosis were reported, and the diagnostic process itself was described as potentially traumatizing.
  • Depression and anxiety were assessed in only 14 of 35 studies, the fewest of the four mental health outcome domains, with mixed results: moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms in 34.0% of 141 patients in China and anxiety in 37.7% of 77 women in Malaysia, but few symptoms among uterus transplant candidates in France.
  • Coping was documented in 15 studies; the strategies reported most often were peer, family, and social support, including online communities (7 studies), and avoidance or concealment (6). Only 2 studies tested a psychosocial intervention.
  • Geography: Europe and Asia contributed 14 studies each, North America 3, Africa and Oceania 1 each, and South America none (2 multinational studies were not assigned to a region).

Public health implications

  • Build psychosocial screening and support into the MRKH diagnostic pathway.
  • Include psychology and social work on multidisciplinary care teams, and pair vaginal lengthening treatment with psychosexual counseling.
  • Train providers in sensitive, non-stigmatizing communication.
  • Expand peer support, and fund research in Africa, South America, and other low-resource settings.

Limitations: English-language database literature from 2019–2026 only (no grey literature); many small, single-center samples with heterogeneous designs and measures; possible overlapping samples; 5 studies assessed from abstracts only; and no quality appraisal (consistent with scoping methods). Counts show how often an outcome was studied, not how common it is.

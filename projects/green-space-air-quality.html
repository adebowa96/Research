Campus Green Space, Air Quality, and Mental Health Among College Students

A narrative review of 15 studies on how polluted air and green space relate to college students’ mental health, and whether campus green space might buffer the effects of air pollution.

Venue
APHA 2026 Annual Meeting and Expo (accepted poster)
Design

Studies

Literature window

Ifeoluwanimi P. Shobayo (first author), Chelsea R. Mazonde, Tahirou Diallo, Fadzai G. Nyarugwe, Benson Varghese, Melissa Lartey, Emmanuel Clottey

Why this question

Depression and anxiety are increasingly common among college students, and universities usually respond with individual-level services such as counseling. The environments students live and study in get less attention. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) have been linked to poorer mental health, while parks, lawns, and tree canopy have been linked to better psychological well-being. Few studies have asked whether green space on campus could offset some of the harm from polluted air.

How does campus green space exposure influence the relationship between air pollution and mental health outcomes among college students?

Methods

  • Design: narrative review of published, peer-reviewed studies. No new data were collected.
  • Search: English-language studies published 2019–2025, using terms such as “air pollution,” “PM2.5,” “green space,” “mental health,” and “college students,” combined with Boolean operators.
  • Eligibility: studies examining air pollution exposure, green space exposure, and mental health outcomes among young adults or university students.
  • Synthesis: titles and abstracts screened, full texts assessed, and included studies summarized narratively.
215 records identified
17 duplicates removed
198 titles and abstracts screened
156 excluded: off-topic, duplicate, or no full text
42 full texts assessed

15 studies included in the narrative synthesis

What the evidence shows

  • Air pollution and mental health: PM2.5, NO2, and PM10 were linked to more symptoms of depression and anxiety and lower life satisfaction among young adults. Proposed pathways include neuroinflammation and oxidative stress.
  • Green space and well-being: exposure to green space was consistently associated with lower stress and better psychological well-being, through restoration, physical activity, and social contact. Vegetation may also capture particulate matter.
  • A possible buffer: emerging studies suggest green space may soften the mental health effects of air pollution, but this interaction has rarely been studied directly in university settings.

Limitations: the review covers 2019–2025 only and may miss earlier foundational work; study designs, populations, and outcome measures varied; and most outcomes were self-reported, with little campus-specific data. A narrative review summarizes the literature; it does not pool effect sizes.

Public health implications

  • Treat campus green infrastructure as an upstream strategy for student mental health, alongside counseling services.
  • Study the interaction between air pollution and green space directly, with longitudinal designs.
  • Evaluate how campus planning and green infrastructure affect air quality and student well-being.

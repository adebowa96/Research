Menstrual Cycle, Stress, and Sleep in Wearable Data

Two analyses of thousands of daily Fitbit records from young adults who menstruate. Linear mixed-effects models account for many days of data from each person.

Courses
PUBH 632 · HLTH 651 Epidemiologic Research Methods
Data
mcPHASES public dataset (42 participants)
Method
Linear mixed-effects regression
Date
April 2026

The data

mcPHASES is a public, de-identified dataset of young adults in the Greater Toronto Area who menstruate. It links daily Fitbit measures with hormone, glucose, and self-reported menstrual-health data across study periods in 2022 and 2024. The unit of analysis is the person-day, and each participant contributes up to several months of days.

Because days from the same person are correlated, ordinary regression would treat them as independent and understate uncertainty. Both analyses use a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for each participant, which accounts for that clustering.

Building the analytic dataset

  • Merged separate source files (activity, sleep score, stress score, hormone and self-report phase labels, participant info) on participant ID, study interval, and study day.
  • Removed duplicate records, keeping one record per person-day.
  • Kept the time order correct: each day's activity was linked to the following night's sleep score.
  • Kept only valid stress readings (excluding failed calculations) and derived age at each study interval.
  • Excluded person-days missing the outcome, exposure, phase, or age.

Analysis 1: Menstrual phase and daily stress

PUBH 632 · 3,732 person-days from 36 participants

Question: Is menstrual cycle phase associated with the daily Fitbit Stress Management Score? The score runs from 1 to 100, and higher scores mean fewer physical signs of stress. The model adjusted for age, weekend versus weekday, and study period, with the follicular phase as the reference.

Phase was associated with stress score overall (p = 0.002). Fertility and luteal days showed more physical signs of stress than follicular days. Older age was also linked to slightly lower scores. In a sensitivity analysis limited to the highest-quality readings, only the fertility-phase difference stayed significant, so it is the most robust finding. A second model with resting heart rate as the outcome showed the same direction of effect.

Analysis 2: Physical activity and sleep

HLTH 651 · 4,893 person-days from 42 participants · analyzed in SAS

Question: Do people sleep better after more active days? The exposure was total active minutes (light + moderate + very active), and the outcome was the next night's Fitbit sleep score. The model adjusted for age and menstrual phase.

Daily activity was not associated with sleep quality: each extra hour of activity changed the sleep score by only about 0.04 points (p = 0.46). Menstrual phase was (p < 0.001).

Interpreting the results carefully

  • Observational data: these are associations, not causes.
  • Proprietary measures: Fitbit's stress and sleep scores come from undisclosed algorithms and are not clinical gold standards.
  • Small, specific cohort: 36–42 young adults from one region, not a representative sample.
  • Phase labels come from cycle and fertility tracking rather than direct confirmation of ovulation.
  • Day-level vs. person-level effects: a next step would separate within-person changes in activity from differences between people.

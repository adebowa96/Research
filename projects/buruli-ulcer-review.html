Antibiotic Therapy for Buruli Ulcer in Ghana

A PRISMA-guided review of how well antibiotic regimens, especially oral rifampicin–clarithromycin, heal Buruli ulcer and prevent recurrence in Ghana.

Course
HLTH 625 Infectious Disease
Method
PRISMA mini systematic review
Studies screened → included
126 → 9
Date
October 2025

Review question

How effective are antibiotic therapies, particularly rifampicin–clarithromycin regimens, at improving clinical outcomes and reducing recurrence of Buruli ulcer in Ghana?

Why it matters

Buruli ulcer, caused by Mycobacterium ulcerans, is a neglected tropical disease that causes disabling skin ulcers, mostly in poor rural communities. West Africa reports most cases worldwide, and Ghana is one of the most affected countries. Treatment has shifted from surgery to antibiotics, and Ghana has adopted an all-oral regimen, but few Ghana-specific reviews have assessed how well it works since then.

Methods

  • Databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, and WHO IRIS, plus reference-list searching.
  • Search string: ("Buruli ulcer" OR "Mycobacterium ulcerans") AND ("antibiotic therapy" OR "rifampicin" OR "clarithromycin" OR "treatment") AND ("clinical outcomes" OR "recurrence") AND ("Ghana").
  • Eligibility: peer-reviewed English-language studies from 2020–2025 reporting at least one clinical outcome (healing, recurrence, or functional recovery).
  • Data extraction: a structured table recording design, setting, sample size, regimen, findings, strengths, limitations, and risk of bias.
  • Synthesis: narrative, because the study designs were too varied for a meta-analysis.

Study selection

126 records identified
37 duplicates removed
89 titles and abstracts screened
55 excluded
34 full texts assessed
25 excluded: not Ghana-specific (8), no treatment data (9), non-human design (5), no full text (3)
9 studies included

Key findings

  • Oral rifampicin–clarithromycin achieved healing rates above 90%, with recurrence below 3%.
  • In a randomized trial, the oral regimen was non-inferior to the older injectable rifampicin–streptomycin regimen and caused fewer side effects.
  • Oral treatment improved adherence, cost, and quality of life.
  • Larger lesions, higher bacterial load, and late diagnosis predicted slower healing.
  • Reports of clarithromycin resistance show that ongoing antimicrobial-resistance surveillance is needed.

Public health recommendations

  • Keep up antibiotic stewardship and resistance monitoring.
  • Strengthen early detection and diagnostic capacity in rural endemic areas.
  • Fund research on shorter, lower-cost regimens.
  • Expand community education so patients seek care earlier.

Cardiovascular Risk Factors in the Framingham Heart Study

An analysis of real, anonymized cohort data from one of the most influential studies in cardiovascular epidemiology: describing the sample, comparing men and women, modeling heart rate, and identifying who has coronary heart disease.

The data

The Framingham Heart Study began in 1948 and has followed three generations of participants to identify the causes of heart disease. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) publishes an anonymized teaching dataset of 4,434 participants with up to three exams each, about six years apart. I analyzed the baseline exam: one record per participant, covering demographics, blood pressure, cholesterol, glucose, smoking, and cardiovascular outcomes.

Men were much more likely to smoke (60% vs. 40%). Women had slightly higher average systolic blood pressure and cholesterol and were twice as likely to be taking blood-pressure medication.

3. What is associated with heart rate?

I fit a simple (crude) linear regression for each characteristic, then one multivariable model including all of them. I restricted the analysis to the 4,026 participants with complete data on every variable.

Diabetes looked strongly linked to heart rate on its own (+4.3 beats/min), but the effect shrank to +0.9 and was no longer significant after adjustment. Most of the crude association was explained by the other variables, especially glucose, which is closely tied to how diabetes is defined.

Men had heart rates about 2.9 beats/min lower than women, and smokers about 2.2 beats/min higher than non-smokers, after adjustment. Age was not associated with heart rate.

Reducing Stress-Related Hypertension Among Police Officers in Ibadan, Nigeria

A theory-based workplace intervention that uses Social Cognitive Theory and the Information–Motivation–Behavioral Skills (IMB) model to help officers manage occupational stress and lower their blood pressure.

Course
HLTH 509 Social & Behavioral Theory
Planning model
PRECEDE assessment
Theories
Self-efficacy (SCT) + IMB model
Date
February–April 2025

The problem

Hypertension is a leading risk factor for stroke, heart attack, and kidney disease. Chronic occupational stress raises that risk, and police officers are among the most exposed workers: they face violence and trauma, work long and irregular shifts that disrupt sleep, and often have limited access to preventive care. Stigma around mental health, and coping through smoking, alcohol, or inactivity, make the problem worse.

I developed this plan over three assignments: a problem statement, a structured outline with a PRECEDE assessment, and a final intervention proposal.

Priority population

Police officers in Ibadan, Nigeria, focusing on high-stress roles such as patrol units, riot control teams, and rapid response squads.

Needs assessment (PRECEDE)

Factor typeWhat drives the problem
Predisposing
individual		Low awareness of stress management; coping through smoking, alcohol, and inactivity; sleep loss from shift work; poor diet
Reinforcing
social		Workplace culture that discourages talking about mental health; peer pressure; limited leadership support for wellness
Enabling
structural		Few structured wellness programs; limited mental-health services; few chances for regular medical check-ups

Theory → intervention

Each program activity is tied to a theoretical construct:

ConstructHow the program applies it
Self-efficacy (SCT)Goal-setting exercises, skill-building workshops, and peer mentorship build officers' confidence that they can manage stress
Observational learning (SCT)Officers learn from peers and supervisors who model healthy stress management
Information (IMB)Education on how stress links to hypertension, and on diet, exercise, and relaxation
Motivation (IMB)Leadership endorsement, peer accountability, recognition and rewards, and personal health goals
Behavioral skills (IMB)Hands-on training in relaxation, fitness, and sleep hygiene

Program activities

  • Physical activity: scheduled aerobic, strength, and resilience training.
  • Stress management training: mindfulness, progressive muscle relaxation, and CBT-based strategies.
  • Peer support networks: structured group discussions and mentorship.
  • Routine screening: regular blood pressure and cardiovascular checks.
  • Workplace wellness education: nutrition, sleep hygiene, and work–life balance.
  • Job redesign: fewer night shifts, less overtime, and rotation out of high-stress duties.
  • Supervisor training: spotting early signs of stress and supporting officers.

Logic model

Resources

Police welfare budgets, NGO and private partners, health professionals, portable BP monitors, education materials, links to local clinics

Short term

Better awareness, stronger stress-management skills, more participation in wellness activities

Intermediate

Fewer stress symptoms, more frequent BP checks, better fitness, healthier lifestyles

Long term

Lower hypertension prevalence, better cardiovascular health, a lasting wellness culture

Evaluation plan

  • Pre/post measures: blood pressure and perceived stress at baseline and after six months.
  • Surveys: validated scales for lifestyle behaviors, coping, and self-efficacy.
  • Biometric monitoring: blood pressure before, during, and after the program.
  • Focus groups: qualitative feedback from officers and supervisors.
  • Participation metrics: session attendance, physical-activity engagement, and screening completion.

Stakeholders and policy recommendations

The coalition includes Nigerian Police Force leadership (implementation), health professionals (screening and care), NGOs and public-health organizations (resources and outreach), the Ministry of Health (policy and funding), and university researchers (evaluation).

  • Mandatory annual hypertension screening for officers.
  • Formal wellness programs built into police training.
  • National guidelines that recognize occupational stress as a risk factor for non-communicable disease among police.
  • Partnerships with local providers for follow-up care, plus sustainable funding.

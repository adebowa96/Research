California’s Maternal Mental Health Screening Law

How California came to require screening for depression and anxiety during and after pregnancy, what the evidence shows since, and why the gap between being screened and getting care is now the law’s main weakness.

Laws
AB 2193 (2018) and AB 1936 (2025)
Framework
Kingdon’s multiple streams
Date
October 2026

The problem

More than one in three Californians who gave birth in 2020–2021 reported depression or anxiety symptoms during pregnancy or after birth, and suicide and drug-related deaths are among the leading causes of postpartum death in the state. AB 2193, in effect since July 2019, requires practitioners who provide prenatal or postpartum care to make sure mothers are offered screening, and requires health plans to run maternal mental health programs. AB 1936, effective January 2025, requires those programs to include screening during pregnancy and within six weeks after birth.

This analysis builds on my CDC PRAMS analysis of perinatal depression in Virginia: that project measured the burden, and this one asks what a screening mandate can and cannot do about it.

How the law was made

Kingdon’s multiple streams framework explains why the bill passed in the form it did:

StreamWhat happened
ProblemMaternal depression and anxiety were common but often missed; a national review estimated that only about 31% of women with postpartum depression were identified.
PolicyScreening tools already tested for accuracy offered a ready solution.
PoliticsThe California Chamber of Commerce opposed the bill over expected premium increases, then dropped its opposition after amendments. The proposed case-management requirement was removed before the governor signed the bill in September 2018.

The result was a compromise: screening became a clear requirement, but case management, the part that helps a mother move from a positive screen to treatment, did not.

What the evidence shows

  • Screening has risen. Self-reported screening during and after pregnancy rose from 51.4% in 2020 to 65.9% in 2024.
  • Treatment lags. Only 47.1% of people reporting depression or anxiety symptoms during or after pregnancy received treatment.
  • Gaps persist inside health systems. In one California health system, documented screening increased after the law, but by 2023 only 57% of patients were screened in both periods.
  • Forecasts are not results. The state’s pre-passage review projected 10,000 more women in treatment, but that estimate assumed case management, which the enacted law left out.

Screening rose after the law took effect, but these before-and-after comparisons cannot show that the law alone caused the change. The health-system finding comes from a single system, and a state ranking of “#1 for maternal mental health” measures policies and services, not whether mothers received effective treatment.

Equity and ethics

  • Unequal reach: in 2024, Black and Hispanic respondents, lower-income households, Medi-Cal members, and people speaking Spanish or other languages at home reported less screening in both periods. These differences show unequal access, not that the law created it.
  • Trust and autonomy: in interviews with 31 postpartum people of color, some hid symptoms for fear of judgment or of losing their children, and others struggled to follow referrals. Mothers need clear explanations of screening, confidentiality, and reporting limits to make informed choices.
  • Unintended consequences: more referrals without more services could lengthen waits, and screening without accessible follow-up can leave mothers frustrated.

The paper frames this through the parable of the Good Samaritan: a screening law helps mothers be seen, but seeing is not the same as caring.

Recommendations

  • Help mothers follow through on referrals, not just receive them.
  • Measure treatment use, symptom improvement, and fair access alongside screening rates.
  • Let mothers’ feedback shape implementation, with attention to affordability and informed choice.

Antibiotic Therapy for Buruli Ulcer in Ghana

A PRISMA-guided review of how well antibiotic regimens, especially oral rifampicin–clarithromycin, heal Buruli ulcer and prevent recurrence in Ghana.

Course
HLTH 625 Prevention and Control of Infectious Disease
Method
PRISMA mini systematic review
Records identified → sources included
126 → 9
Date
October 2025

Review question

How effective are antibiotic therapies, particularly rifampicin–clarithromycin regimens, at improving clinical outcomes and reducing recurrence of Buruli ulcer in Ghana?

Why it matters

Buruli ulcer, caused by Mycobacterium ulcerans, is a neglected tropical disease that causes disabling skin ulcers, mostly in poor rural communities. West Africa reports most cases worldwide, and Ghana is one of the most affected countries. Treatment has shifted from surgery to antibiotics, and Ghana has adopted an all-oral regimen, but few Ghana-specific reviews have assessed how well it works since then.

Methods

  • Databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, and WHO IRIS, plus reference-list searching.
  • Search string: ("Buruli ulcer" OR "Mycobacterium ulcerans") AND ("antibiotic therapy" OR "rifampicin" OR "clarithromycin" OR "treatment") AND ("clinical outcomes" OR "recurrence") AND ("Ghana").
  • Eligibility: peer-reviewed English-language primary studies conducted in Ghana (2020–2025) reporting at least one clinical outcome (healing, recurrence, or functional recovery).
  • Data extraction: a structured table recording design, setting, sample size, regimen, findings, strengths, limitations, and a high/moderate/low quality rating.
  • Synthesis: narrative, because the study designs were too varied for a meta-analysis.

Study selection

126 records identified
37 duplicates removed
89 titles and abstracts screened
55 excluded
34 full texts assessed
25 excluded: not Ghana-specific (8), no treatment data (9), non-human design (5), no full text (3)
9 sources included: 5 Ghanaian clinical studies + 4 supporting papers

Looking back: the four supporting papers (two reviews, a laboratory study, and a trial protocol) don't meet the review's own primary-study criteria. A stricter synthesis would rest on the five Ghanaian clinical studies, which is how I'd structure it today.

Key findings

  • Included studies reported healing rates above 90% with rifampicin–clarithromycin (in the trial, among patients with small, early lesions), and national surveillance of antibiotic-treated patients reported recurrence below 3%.
  • In an open-label randomized trial among patients with small, early lesions, the oral regimen was non-inferior to the older injectable rifampicin–streptomycin regimen and caused fewer side effects.
  • Studies reported easier adherence and lower costs with oral treatment, and an observational study linked it to better function and quality of life, although those comparisons were against historical injectable-treated patients rather than randomized.
  • Larger lesions and greater bacterial diversity in wounds were associated with slower healing, and delayed diagnosis was cited as a contributing factor.
  • A national One Health review of antimicrobial resistance in Ghana (not specific to M. ulcerans) reported clarithromycin resistance, which argues for continued resistance surveillance alongside the oral regimen.

Public health recommendations

  • Keep up antibiotic stewardship and resistance monitoring.
  • Strengthen early detection and diagnostic capacity in rural endemic areas.
  • Fund research on shorter, lower-cost regimens.
  • Expand community education so patients seek care earlier.

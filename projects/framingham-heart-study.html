Cardiovascular Risk Factors in the Framingham Heart Study

An analysis of real, anonymized cohort data from one of the most influential studies in cardiovascular epidemiology: describing the sample, comparing men and women, modeling heart rate, and identifying who has coronary heart disease.

Data
NHLBI Framingham teaching dataset
Participants
4,434 (baseline exam)
Methods & tool
t-tests, linear regression, cross-tabulation · Excel

The data

The Framingham Heart Study began in 1948 and has followed three generations of participants to identify the causes of heart disease. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) publishes an anonymized teaching dataset of 4,434 participants with up to three exams each, about six years apart. I analyzed the baseline exam: one record per participant, covering demographics, blood pressure, cholesterol, glucose, smoking, and cardiovascular outcomes. Outcome variables record whether a condition, such as hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or stroke, was already present at baseline or occurred during up to 24 years of follow-up.

3. What is associated with heart rate?

I fit a simple (crude) linear regression for each characteristic, then one multivariable model including all of them. I restricted the analysis to the 4,026 participants with complete data on every variable (408 excluded, mostly for missing glucose values).

Diabetes looked strongly linked to heart rate on its own (+4.3 beats/min), but the association shrank to +0.9 and was no longer statistically significant after adjustment (p = 0.52). Most of that crude association was most likely absorbed by glucose, which overlaps heavily with diabetes because diabetes is partly defined by high glucose.

Men had heart rates about 2.9 beats/min lower than women, and smokers about 2.2 beats/min higher than non-smokers, after adjustment. Age showed no statistically significant association with heart rate.

4. Who has coronary heart disease?

I compared participants who already had coronary heart disease (CHD) at the baseline exam with those who did not, using two-sample t-tests.

People with CHD were about eight years older on average and had clearly higher blood pressure and BMI. Their cholesterol was also higher, but the difference was borderline (p = 0.049 with a pooled-variance t-test). Because this compares people who already had CHD, it shows which characteristics go along with the disease, not what caused it.

Why a cohort design?

I also evaluated how the study's design affects what the data can show. A long-term cohort records risk factors before outcomes happen, so it can measure incidence and many outcomes at once. The trade-offs are cost, participant dropout over time, and, as in any observational design, confounding. A case-control study would be faster and cheaper, but it is more vulnerable to recall bias and selection bias. For Framingham's goals, the cohort design is the better fit.
